**Perkly** is a fictional deals marketplace, invented for this exercise. The question: how do you build the system that decides what each customer sees, makes it smarter every time they come back, and shows it is actually making money?

<a href="/perkly/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">**Open the live prototype ↗**</a> — a phone-sized Explore feed, with the customer profile, event log and ranking trace updating beside it. All deals, merchants, margins and model coefficients are synthetic demo data.

## The problem

Millions of customers a month, hundreds of thousands of deals across local services, things to do, travel and goods, three customer segments — and yet a single experience for all of them.

- **Goals:** grow margin, and get more customers to buy again within 30 days.
- **Capacity:** three engineers, half a data scientist, half a designer.
- **In short:** build the system that decides what each customer sees, make it smarter every time they come back, and show how we would know it is making money.

## Three segments, and what to learn about each

Three segments that matter to the business, map to feature-rich deal categories, and have enough volume to train a model on. For all of them the platform already has basic demographics, searches, views, purchase and redemption history, and merchant locations. What is missing is preference — and the answer is to learn it from behaviour rather than ask for it.

**Travel seekers**
- Preference axes: budget ↔ premium · day trip ↔ long stay · close to home ↔ far away · relax ↔ culture and sights · beach.
- Learned from behaviour: destination type, trip and accommodation type, stars, board, season, family or couples-friendly, flight, spa or pool, cancellation terms.

**Beauty and wellness**
- Preference axes: budget ↔ premium · pampering ↔ active · at a venue ↔ comes to you · standard ↔ luxury · proximity.
- Learned from behaviour: treatment type and area, facility type and facilities, indoor or outdoor, individual or group, couples-friendly, service level, brand, appointment flexibility.

**Food and drink**
- Preference axes: budget ↔ premium · everyday ↔ special occasion · just the meal ↔ class, tour or tasting · solo or couple ↔ group · proximity.
- Learned from behaviour: cuisine, meal and venue type, occasion, atmosphere, price level, menu, dietary options, drinks included, indoor or outdoor, reservation.

**Signals that apply to every segment:** price elasticity, discount percentage, rating and reviews, popularity, distance, gift intent, satiation (a similar purchase made recently) and ignored impressions.

**Start with food and drink.** Frequent, repeatable purchases give the fastest read on 30-day repeat, and the beta risk is contained.

## How the system works

</a>

*Click the diagram to open it full size.*

A customer opens the app, new or returning. The backend then does five things in real time:

1. **Identify the customer and load their profile** from an online profile store. With no profile yet, it falls back to a cold-start prior for the segment, plus whatever the current session already shows.
2. **Retrieve eligible deal candidates** — filter by availability, location and business rules, and apply any search or filters.
3. **Estimate purchase propensity** — a trained model (gradient-boosted trees) combines profile, deal, context and session signals.
4. **Apply expected margin rate** — fetch the expected margin per deal, include promo economics and business rules, and combine it with propensity into an expected value.
5. **Rank and return the page** — score is propensity × expected margin rate, then diversity and freshness rules, and the top deals go back with an explanation the UI can show.

Four sources feed it: **customer behaviour** (events streamed from the app), the **deal catalog**, **business economics** (revenue, merchant payout, promo funding — from the finance warehouse) and the **current session context**. Every impression and interaction is logged and streamed back: the customer profile updates in real time, and an offline pipeline retrains the model on the history and ships the new version.

## The first three months

- **Month 1 — foundations.** Extract deal feature sets, audit event logging including impressions, and baseline the current Explore ranking.
- **Month 2 — first model.** Train a propensity model, build a margin-aware ranking service, and evaluate offline against the baseline.
- **Month 3 — beta.** A/B test in the starting segment, with guardrail dashboards and alerts and a weekly retraining pipeline.

**Deliberately later:** natural-language search (it needs a proven ranker and more design capacity) and personalised pricing (margin and trust risk).

**Team split:** engineer 1 on data and features, engineer 2 on the ranking service, engineer 3 on the app and experiments, the data scientist on the model and evaluation, the designer on the Explore UI.

## How we would know it works

- **Approach:** A/B test against the current Explore experience.
- **Primary outcomes:** realised gross margin rate (take rate as a proxy) and 30-day repeat purchase rate, confirmed by research.
- **Guardrails — must not drop:** overall purchase volume and revenue, and customer satisfaction.
- **Stop criteria:** stop if a key guardrail falls beyond a pre-defined threshold, or if deal relevance is clearly worse.
- **Before rollout:** validate ranking relevance and the UX internally, then run a controlled beta against a control group.

## Challenging the plan

**Pre-mortem.** Six months in, it has failed: we were not able to match what customers prefer to the deals we show efficiently enough. The experience did not improve — it only became harder to maintain. The two things that would make that likely: a small team, and margin-seeking that skews the results.

**Contrarian view.** "More personalisation is always better" is wrong for a marketplace people visit to find something new. Many come to step out of the ordinary, and over-personalising turns the feed into more of the same. So serendipity has to stay in the design: reserve roughly 15% of slots for exploration (new merchants, local trends), track discovery as the share of purchases in a new category, and treat serendipity as a feature rather than noise.
